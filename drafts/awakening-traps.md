We need more guidance for integrating spiritual awakening into daily life.

People encounter oneness, undergo ego death, feel the presence of god, experience kundalini awakening, or enter the mysteries through entheogens—and then have to figure out how to integrate those experiences. Entheogens are substances used to facilitate spiritual experiences.

People can begin an awakening process and become destabilized without mentorship. We need elders, mentors, and communities that can help us understand what we experience and apply what we learn in our relationships, work, and care for our bodies.

During awakening, we can become more aware and sensitive, including to emotional wounds and beliefs we do not yet understand. We need to develop our ability to examine these experiences, understand our reactions, and integrate what we learn.

These are some difficulties I wish I had guidance on during my own awakening.

## 1. Neglecting physical needs

As subtle and spiritual worlds become more vivid, ordinary physical existence can start to feel burdensome. Why deal with dishes, money, maintenance, or the body when you can commune with the infinite?

That pull deserves attention. Sometimes what feels like transcendence contains an urge to escape the difficulty of being here.

The body belongs to the mystery too. So do food, sleep, shelter, movement, and the work of keeping things alive. A garden requires tending even after you recognize god in every leaf.

We live oneness through particular bodies, with particular needs and responsibilities. Integrating what we discover includes attending to those needs and responsibilities.

Eat, rest, and maintain what your life depends on.

## 2. Accepting experience without evaluating behavior

After an experience of unity, you may reconsider old judgments. You may begin to recognize the suffering behind behavior you once condemned, or understand a worldview that previously seemed incomprehensible.

But understanding a behavior does not make it ethical. Ultimately, all is one, but we also live with particular circumstances and physical limits. We can only do so much. Ethics and wisdom matter in how we choose to act. Compassion does not make every action equally wise.

You can recognize someone’s divinity, acknowledge the harm they cause, and hold them accountable.

Loving someone includes discerning how their behavior affects others. Otherwise, saying “everything is valid” can become a way to abandon people who need protection—including yourself.

## 3. Rejecting or destroying existing systems

Once you begin seeing exploitation and suffering in existing systems, you may feel inclined to rebel against, reject, or even destroy the systems causing that pain. That impulse can extend to everything associated with them: money, institutions, medicine, law, commerce, civilization itself.

Grief and anger are warranted. But honoring every experience does not make every interpretation true, or every response equally effective. If we desire less suffering, we need to examine whether our actions actually reduce it.

When we define ourselves through opposition, we can remain preoccupied with what we reject. When our identity becomes organized around fighting an enemy, we risk reproducing its patterns: domination in the name of liberation, exclusion in the name of purity, coercion in the name of love. Within oneness, what we reject still belongs to the whole. In daily life, we remain connected through habits, dependencies, relationships, and emotional wounds. Declaring ourselves separate does not end those connections.

Become curious about the “bad” thing. Try to understand it from within: what needs sustain it, what pressures shape the people inside it, and where its workings contradict its stated purpose. Use that understanding to decide what to preserve, what to change, and how to build a new system without recreating the same harms.

Money, for example, can be understood as a shared language for exchanging human energy, labor, and resources. Scrutinize the particular dynamics of debt, interest, derivatives, or inflation rather than treating exchange itself as the source of harm. What arrangements extract value, concentrate power, or shift costs onto others? What would allow people to coordinate and exchange more fairly? An alternative still needs collectively compatible ways to meet those needs.

The same discernment applies to healthcare, law, and other inherited institutions. Abruptly rejecting their support can add unnecessary suffering before an alternative is ready. Use what sustains you while developing greater self-sufficiency and reciprocal support. Use the freedom you gain to build new collective systems. Interdependence remains part of the work.

Sometimes resistance, refusal, and dismantling are necessary. Plan for how people will meet their needs during the transition. If the urge to “burn it all down” is overwhelming, take time to grieve the very real pain of living within exploitative systems. Seek care for that grief while also considering what a viable alternative requires.

Direct your effort toward building the conditions for the life you desire.

## 4. Assuming an entity’s agenda aligns with yours

You may encounter gods, ancestors, spirits, guides, extraterrestrial beings, or other intelligences. Their knowledge, power, or capabilities can make it tempting to assume they also know what is best for you.

Keep your discernment. Greater knowledge or more advanced technology does not establish an alignment of agendas. Ask what the entity desires, what you desire, and what your exchange creates together.

An agenda is what a being desires and intends to pursue. Different aims can be compatible without being identical, and an exchange can be symbiotic: everyone involved benefits. Within oneness, we discover and develop ourselves through relationships. Ask whether this particular relationship supports the experience and growth you actually desire.

A guide might delight in introducing you to new experiences while you desire help integrating experiences you have already had. Neither desire needs to be wrong for the timing or exchange to be mismatched. More revelation is not always what you are asking for.

The same discernment applies to teachers, healers, channelers, communities, and even your own internal parts—the different motivations and perspectives you find within yourself. What do we each desire from this exchange? What do we want to develop together? What pace, boundaries, and forms of participation support that? If you do not know what you desire, it becomes easier to enter an experience you did not anticipate. Take time to discover and express your desires, including where you are still unsure.

Respect for boundaries is an expression of love. When fear arises, there should be room to pause, find reassurance, and choose whether to continue. A request to stop deserves respect. The trickster has a place in the mysteries too: through light-hearted mischief, a trickster can challenge your certainty and help you grow. But the promise of growth does not override your right to stop; you can welcome surprise without consenting to every form it takes.

You do not need to settle the ultimate nature of an encounter before deciding whether to follow its guidance. When a source’s purposes are unclear, leave room for uncertainty rather than assuming either benevolence or hostility. Let trust grow with evidence of compatibility, reciprocity, and respect for your agency.

There should always be room to update the agreement or opt out. Changing course can be challenging, especially when attachment, habit, or obligation has formed. That difficulty does not make an agreement irrevocable. Your participation can change as your desires and understanding change.

## 5. Confusing revelation with interpretation

You may receive a download: information experienced as images, sensations, symbols, or direct knowing before you can put it into words. Give yourself time to receive it before deciding exactly what it means.

We often rush to fit new information into something familiar. We interpret through unconscious beliefs, fears, and desires, sometimes so quickly that we mistake our interpretation for part of the original download. Remaining open to different interpretations helps us notice what we received and what we subsequently added.

In remote-viewing training, practitioners use the term analytical overlay for interpretations drawn from memory and imagination that they add to the impressions they record. The practical discipline is to record the impressions, if possible, before rushing to identify them.

Sometimes you just *know*, with a clarity very different from what you experienced before. Later, during ordinary routines, you may begin to doubt that knowing. You can remember the magic and the clarity of the experience while remaining open to how you understand it.

When you become too certain, you may stop considering other possibilities. Leave room for mystery. Learn to love the unknown and discover things you would never have anticipated. You can trust yourself and still revise an interpretation as you learn more.

## 6. Omitting action or judging delays in manifestation

I approach manifestation as a practice with specific steps. In Bashar’s [seven steps of manifestation](https://www.bashar.org/sevensteps), you practice vision, desire, belief, acceptance, intent, action, and allowance:

1. envision what you desire
2. feel your excitement for it
3. believe in its possibility
4. accept your capacity to participate
5. form the intention
6. embody it in action
7. allow the outcome to unfold

One difficulty is doing the internal work while leaving physical action out of the practice. If you desire new encounters but never vary where you go or what you do, you limit the range of encounters available to you. Go somewhere new. Accept an invitation. Begin a conversation. By acting, you can encounter more possibilities.

Practice manifestation through your beliefs and physical participation. Remain receptive to synchronicity—meaningfully connected events—including outcomes you could never have planned. By participating in more situations, you have more opportunities to experience such connections. Taking action is part of choosing the reality you experience.

Another difficulty is turning the lag between intention and manifestation into a judgment about yourself. An outcome need not arrive immediately. You may repeatedly think, “It hasn’t happened, so something must be wrong with my consciousness,” then monitor and judge yourself more harshly. Give yourself time without treating the delay as a measure of your worth.

I understand co-creation, or creating reality together, through a marketplace analogy. You place a bid through your intention, with its weight determined by your willpower, clarity of desire, and freedom from attachment. In this model, you automatically communicate that intention across the network of all consciousness in reality. Beings with compatible desires can accept and participate. Many beings can participate at once and fulfill different intentions through the same events.

I draw a related analogy from [polycomputing](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-7673/8/1/110), as described by Bongard and Levin: the same physical material or system can perform multiple computations simultaneously, with different observers drawing different useful results from it. In co-creation, different beings can fulfill different purposes and experience different meanings through the same event.

You may experience manifestation through a sequence of synchronicities, with each event affecting what becomes possible next. Keep participating, clarify your intention, and remain open to different ways of fulfilling it.

## 7. Isolating as sensitivity increases

As your sensitivity to subtle energies deepens, certain environments, relationships, and forms of stimulation may become harder to tolerate.

Negativity, violence, or manipulation may cause more discomfort than before.

You may value your increased sensitivity and still find it difficult to participate in daily life when so much feels painful.

You can develop both discernment and capacity to remain sensitive while engaging with others.

- Discernment is recognizing which situations align with your desires and limits. Notice when they do not, and act on that understanding.
- Capacity is your ability to remain present with difficult emotions, conflict, or disruptive energies. Developing it can reduce your need to isolate. When you react strongly to a situation, examine both what is happening and your response. You may be reacting through unresolved pain, responding to present harm, or experiencing both. Being affected does not automatically mean something is wrong within you.

Leave when you need to. Rest when you need to. Explore what an experience reveals without making yourself responsible for everything that hurts you.

## 8. Using spiritual explanations to avoid grief or accountability

“Everything happens for a reason.”

“We are all one.”

“The soul chose this.”

You may say these things from sincere spiritual understanding. You can also use them to end a conversation when someone needs to express grief or anger, or seek accountability. Using spiritual explanations to avoid such needs is often called spiritual bypassing.

You can believe in a soul’s journey and still mourn what happened. You can recognize the divine in someone and demand that they stop hurting people. Forgiveness does not require renewed access.

Include grief, anger, and accountability in your spiritual understanding.

Some things need tears, repair, restitution, or a clear refusal. Explaining them from eternity does not complete that work.

## 9. Identifying as spiritually superior

An experience of ego death can become the foundation of a new identity: the awakened one, the healer, the chosen messenger, the person who sees what everyone else cannot.

A calling can become entangled with the need to be exceptional. Disagreement starts to look like proof that others are asleep. Ordinary feedback becomes something you believe you have outgrown.

Watch what happens when your spiritual identity is questioned. Can you remain curious? Can you apologize? Can you learn from someone who does not share your vocabulary?

Inherent divinity belongs to the person who disagrees with us too.

When you accept a calling, you also accept responsibility for how you act on it.

## 10. Seeking peak experiences without integrating them

A profound ceremony or mystical opening can become the standard against which ordinary life feels inadequate.

Then comes the pursuit of another breakthrough: another ceremony, another initiation, another dissolution, another revelation powerful enough to restore the sense of arrival.

Consider what you have changed in your life since the last opening. What have you practiced? Which relationships have you attended to? What have you left unfinished while seeking another answer?

Give yourself time to integrate between revelations. Consider whether the urgency to have another experience reflects what you need.

In myths of initiation, the traveler returns home and puts what they learned into practice within their community. Consider how you can apply what you have learned in your own life and share it with others.

## 11. Assuming total control or total powerlessness

You may swing between two extremes.

In one, you are the creator of everything, responsible for every circumstance through your consciousness. In the other, you are a helpless cog in systems too vast to influence.

Both obscure the scale at which you can act.

You participate in creation alongside other beings, histories, institutions, and material conditions. Your choices matter within that web, though they do not command every outcome.

Ask what is yours to change, what requires cooperation, and what you must presently live with.

Take responsibility for what you can influence, and recognize where you need to cooperate with others or accept limits.

## 12. Trying to save others without their consent

A calling to serve can become an obligation to awaken everyone, heal everyone, or rescue people who have not asked for your help.

You may offer insight faster than someone can receive it, interpret their resistance as spiritual blindness, or exhaust yourself trying to complete another person’s journey.

Service requires consent. People have their own timing, language, relationships, and ways of finding meaning.

Offer what you have the capacity to share freely, while continuing to meet your own needs. Listen before interpreting. Allow others to decline.

If you believe you need to save the world, you may underestimate others’ ability to act for themselves. What would you do if you found out the world was already saved? Consider whether you could express more love through that activity than through taking responsibility for everyone else.

## 13. Expecting awakening to end personal difficulties

You may believe that awakening should end confusion, conflict, grief, or the need for help.

Then ordinary difficulty becomes evidence of spiritual failure. You conceal what you are struggling with because it threatens the story of who you have become.

Leave room to remain human.

You may feel you have awakened and completed the work, then notice subtler difficulties as your sensitivity increases. Major realizations and lasting changes are possible. Pain may become less intense, and you may experience more play and enjoyment. You can also revisit familiar difficulties with greater awareness and new ways of responding.

You can encounter god and still need guidance. You can experience oneness and still have an argument to repair. You can undergo a profound initiation and spend years learning how to embody what it showed you.

## Finding guidance and community

As more people explore and deepen their conscious awareness, I want guidance like this to be easier to find. These experiences are ordinary parts of human life that often go unspoken. We need places where we can speak openly about them, compare experiences, ask questions, and help each other integrate.

When these experiences go unspoken, someone can encounter them without ever having heard others describe something similar. It can be frightening and lonely without language, guidance, or someone to talk to. We need opportunities to explore the spiritual meaning of an experience while also caring for practical needs. We can revise our explanations of reality as we learn from the people living through it.

I hope we can make these conversations ordinary enough that people know where to turn. Somewhere you can say, “Something happened, and I don’t know how to understand it,” and find people willing to listen, share what has helped them, and explore with you.

We still have a lot to discover, through scientific inquiry and through sharing and examining our experiences. I’d love for us to discover more of reality together.
