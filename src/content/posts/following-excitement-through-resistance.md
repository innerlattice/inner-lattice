Aya's process is a loop for the moments when you notice that you are not in [flow](#g-natural-flow), or that you feel stuck or frustrated. Each term with a specific meaning links to its definition in the [glossary](#glossary) at the end.

## 1. The process

1. **Check your excitement.** Ask: am I following my [highest excitement](#g-highest-excitement) right now?
   - **a. No, and nothing resists.** Do the most exciting thing, from [natural flow](#g-natural-flow) rather than from [attachment](#g-attachment) or [aversion](#g-aversion). You are done.
   - **b. No, because something resists.** Notice what [resistance](#g-resistance) arises when you go to do it. Go to step 3.
   - **c. Yes, but something bothers me.** Name the specific want that feels [stuck or frustrated](#g-stuck-or-frustrated). Go to step 2.
2. **Widen the options.** Use techniques for [considering exciting potentials](#g-considering-potentials) outside your [possibility space](#g-possibility-space).
   - **a. You have no wish to consider anything new.** Ask: "What [part](#g-part) of me feels it's beneficial to stay the same?" Go to step 3.
   - **b. Nothing excites you.** [Feel somatically](#g-feeling-somatically) for a [fear-based belief](#g-fear-based-belief) that limits you. Flatness here is more often [hidden fear](#g-hidden-fear) than a real lack of excitement. Go to step 3.
   - **c. A new potential excites you.** Do the most exciting one. Go to step 1.
3. **Meet the part.** Thank the resisting [part](#g-part) for [doing its best to protect you](#g-protective-intent). Then hold a [curiosity-based dialogue](#g-curiosity-based-dialogue) with it to understand why it resists, or why its behavior seems beneficial to it. Continue until one of these comes up, then go to step 4.
   - **a. A [past traumatic memory](#g-past-traumatic-memory).** Use an [integration technique](#g-integration-technique): [memory reconsolidation](#g-memory-reconsolidation), [gentle reparenting](#g-gentle-reparenting), or both.
   - **b. [Anxiety](#g-anxiety).** If the anxiety is not [distorted](#g-distorted-anxiety), listen to it and do what it indicates, and repeat step 3 with the part that resists doing it.
   - **c. [Fear](#g-fear).** [Feel the fear fully](#g-feeling-the-fear-fully), and [titrate](#g-titration) if it is too strong to feel all at once. When it subsides, use an [integration technique](#g-integration-technique).
4. **Check for integration.** Does the part feel [acknowledged](#g-acknowledged), and have you had a [realization](#g-realization) or an [integration](#g-integration)?
   - **a. Not yet.** Go to step 3.
   - **b. Not yet, and you need to meet a [direct need](#g-direct-needs).** Thank the part, and [commit to return](#g-commit-to-return) to it later if it still feels unresolved. Go to step 1.
   - **c. Yes.** Go to step 2.

![The four steps of the process arranged in a loop, with natural flow as the only exit, and each branch drawn as an arrow from the step where it starts](../../assets/diagrams/resistance-process.svg "Natural flow is the only exit. Every other branch leads through a part, and every integration leads back to a wider set of options.")

Steps 1 and 2 concern what you could do. Steps 3 and 4 concern what stops you. In the loop, you never override a part: when a part resists, you go to meet it, and you return to action only once it has been heard. Integration leads back to step 2 rather than step 1, because a resolved belief often makes options visible that were not there before.

## 2. Excitement as the signal

The phrase *highest excitement* comes from Bashar, a teacher channeled by Darryl Anka. In his formula, you act on your highest excitement to the best of your ability, with integrity, and without insisting on a particular outcome.[[1]](#ref-1) You do not have to accept Bashar's metaphysics to use the instruction. As a working hypothesis, the instruction amounts to this: the strongest felt pull of interest in the present moment is better information about what to do next than a plan that an earlier version of you made with less information. You can test the hypothesis directly. For a week, whenever you are free to choose among several things, choose the one that feels most alive, and notice whether your energy rises or falls over the following hours.

Highest excitement is relative to the options in front of you, so it is not always thrilling. Cleaning a toilet can be your highest excitement. So can rest. What makes a choice your highest excitement is that you choose it for its own sake, because it carries the strongest pull among the things available to you now.

The difficulty is that two other forces produce pulls that feel similar. Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* list attachment (*raga*) and aversion (*dvesha*) among the five afflictions (*kleshas*): attachment follows the memory of pleasure, and aversion follows the memory of pain.[[2]](#ref-2) Buddhism names greed (*lobha*) and aversion (*dosa*), with delusion (*moha*), as the three roots of unwholesome action.[[3]](#ref-3) The *Bhagavad Gita* traces how dwelling on an object produces attachment, attachment produces desire, and frustrated desire produces anger and confusion.[[4]](#ref-4) In all three traditions, a pull rooted in a belief about what you must have or must avoid differs from a pull that comes from interest.

The difference can be felt. Attachment has a grasping, compulsive quality, as if something terrible will happen if you do not get the thing. Aversion pushes you away from one option, and anything that offers escape can then look attractive. Excitement needs no particular outcome. If you choose something mainly to escape something else, the choice comes from aversion, however exciting it feels. The essay on [consideration space](/consideration-space#attachment-and-aversion) describes how attachment and aversion also narrow the options you can consider at all.

People commonly misread this signal in two ways. In achievement culture, people treat excitement as an indulgence and discipline as the virtue, and they learn to override what they feel. In some spiritual cultures, people treat every desire as a problem to renounce, and they learn to distrust what they feel. The process takes a third position: excitement is information about where your energy can flow without conflict, and resistance is information about a part of you that has not yet agreed.

## 3. Where resistance comes from

Resistance is any felt reluctance, contraction, avoidance, or loss of energy that comes up when you go to act on a want. The process assumes that resistance always comes from a belief, and that the belief usually formed from experience long before you could examine it.

Research on emotional learning describes how this happens. An emotionally significant event becomes associated with the cues and contexts around it. Learning then generalizes to cues that resemble the original ones, and through abstraction the shared features of many such episodes become a schema: a ready-made interpretation that sets how you think, feel, and act in new situations. Schemas make fast responses possible. A schema becomes maladaptive when it applies an aversive response to situations that no longer call for it.[[5]](#ref-5) Fear generalization is adaptive in moderation, and overgeneralization is a consistent finding in anxiety disorders.[[6]](#ref-6) Generalization also spreads along lines of meaning. In one experiment, people who learned to fear a single cue came to respond with fear to new members of a whole conceptual category that the cue had been linked to.[[7]](#ref-7) One painful experience of being criticized for speaking up can become a general expectation that speaking up is dangerous, and several such experiences can make that expectation feel like simple fact.

That is why the beliefs behind resistance rarely feel like beliefs. They feel like how things are. They also change what you perceive as possible: a belief that asking for help leads to rejection removes asking for help from the set of options you consider. The essay on [consideration space](/consideration-space) maps this set in detail. In this process it is called the *possibility space*.

Klaus Scherer's component process model of emotion explains why you often feel resistance before you can name its belief. In the model, an emotion is an episode of synchronized change across several components of the organism: the appraisal of an event, physiological arousal, motor expression, action tendencies, and subjective feeling. The appraisal runs as a sequence of checks on whether the event is relevant to you, what it implies for your goals, whether you can cope with it, and whether it fits your norms and self-image, and much of this checking happens automatically.[[8]](#ref-8) A belief changes the appraisal, and the appraisal changes your body and your impulses, before any of it reaches words. Tightness in the chest or a sudden urge to check your phone may be the first sign that a part has appraised an option as a threat. This is why the process asks you to feel somatically before you analyze. In *Focusing*, Eugene Gendlin teaches the same move: attend to the vague bodily *felt sense* of a situation until words come that fit it.[[9]](#ref-9)

In the process, flatness gets the same treatment. When nothing excites you, the cause may be a lack of interest, but it is more often a fear you do not feel directly. The fear-based belief removes the appealing options before you can consider them, and what you experience is the absence: numbness, boredom, or a sense that nothing matters.

### Fragmentation

Hegel located the root of the problem one level deeper. In his first published book, he argued that philosophy becomes necessary when the unifying power in human life weakens and its opposites harden into separate, independent things: "Dichotomy is the source of the need of philosophy."[[10]](#ref-10) In the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, he describes the "unhappy consciousness," a consciousness that experiences itself as divided against itself and suffers from the division.[[11]](#ref-11)

Read psychologically, Hegel's point fits inner conflict closely. A part that resists is a piece of you that split off at some point, with feelings and beliefs at odds with the rest of you. The suffering comes from the split, which makes you act from two places at once. So the remedy is to reunite the part with the whole, which is what this process calls integration. Defeating the part, or arguing it out of its position, would only widen the split, because the part would still hold its belief and you would now be fighting it.

## 4. Parts

Richard Schwartz developed Internal Family Systems (IFS) after his clients kept describing their inner lives as conversations among distinct parts. In IFS, the mind is naturally multiple. Some parts protect proactively, by managing your behavior so that old pain never gets triggered. Others protect reactively, by distracting or numbing you once it has been triggered. Still others carry the pain itself, from experiences that the protectors work to keep out of awareness. Beneath the parts is what Schwartz calls the Self, which has qualities such as curiosity, calm, and compassion, and which can relate to each part without taking its side.[[12]](#ref-12) The title of his most accessible book states the central claim: *No Bad Parts*. Every part has a positive intent, even when its strategy causes harm.

This is why step 3 starts with thanks. A protector that is attacked defends its position harder, and a protector that is thanked can relax enough to explain itself. You do not have to believe that parts are little people inside you for this to work. In his account of IFS, Kaj Sotala starts from the design of a robot that has to avoid catastrophes, and he shows that ideas from machine learning and neuroscience lead to a mind made of subsystems, each of which learned to predict and prevent a particular bad outcome and competes for control of attention and action.[[13]](#ref-13) Talking to such a subsystem as if it were a person is an interface that works, because the subsystem holds a model of a person's situation and responds to updates about it.

A curiosity-based dialogue has no agenda except understanding. Find where the resistance sits in your body, and ask it questions. What are you afraid would happen if you stopped? What are you trying to get for me? How long have you been doing this job? How old do you think I am? Then wait for the answer, which may arrive as words, an image, a memory, or a shift in sensation. Do not argue with what you hear. If you notice an urge to convince the part, that urge belongs to another part, and you can thank it and ask it to step back. When two parts want opposite things, Internal Double Crux, a technique from the Center for Applied Rationality, is a structured way for the two parts to argue until each has heard the other's real concern.[[14]](#ref-14)

With the question in step 2a, "What part of me feels it's beneficial to stay the same?", you look for the protector of the status quo. When you have no wish to consider anything new, some part has usually decided that new options are dangerous, and asking the question brings it into the conversation instead of leaving it to stop the search unnoticed.

## 5. Anxiety and fear

Anxiety and fear both arise in step 3, and you meet them differently.

### Anxiety

Anxiety involves a prediction: either that something bad will happen in the future, or that something bad from the past is about to happen again. Either way, it directs your attention away from the present moment. Elizabeth April treats anxiety as a signal that points to unresolved emotional and spiritual issues, and as something that can become an ally to your intuition once you learn to read it.[[15]](#ref-15)

The process listens to anxiety, but checks it first. Anxiety is distorted when it is out of proportion to a probable threat, and four questions test for distortion. Is the anxiety about this exact scenario, or about a similar scenario from the past? Does its intensity match the consequences if the bad scenario came true? Can you control the scenario at all? If you act to reduce the risk, does the anxiety fall in proportion?

If the anxiety passes these tests, it usually concerns a concrete action you can take, such as checking the contract, making the call, or copying your files to a backup. Take that action. If some part of you resists doing it, that resistance is a new part to meet, and step 3 begins again with it. If the anxiety is distorted, its source is usually a memory rather than the present situation, and the part that holds it can be met like any other.

Anxiety can turn into a powerful intuition if you cultivate a good relationship with it. Each time you listen to it, check it, and act on it when it is accurate, you learn more about what it is reporting. With practice, you can tell quickly which warnings concern the present and which concern the past, and the warnings that concern the present become guidance you can trust.

### Fear

Fear is more immediate than anxiety. It is a felt sense of threat in the body, and the usual response is to avoid it: think about something else, keep busy, or do anything that makes the sensation stop. Avoidance works in the short term and keeps the fear intact in the long term, because the part that holds it never learns that the feeling can be survived.

The alternative is to let the fear run its full course in your body while you stay present with it. Teal Swan teaches this as an emotional practice: turn toward the feeling, let it be as strong as it is, and stay with it instead of trying to control it. She argues that emotions that are not fully felt persist as triggers.[[16]](#ref-16) Fear that is fully felt rises, peaks, and subsides, often faster than you expect. When it subsides, the belief behind it is still there, so step 3c continues with an integration technique.

Some fear is too large to feel all at once. If staying with it leads to panic, numbness, or a sense of leaving your body, you have taken too large a dose. In Somatic Experiencing, Peter Levine handles this with *titration* and *pendulation*: you touch a small part of the activation, then move your attention to a resource, such as a place in your body that feels calm, then return to the activation, so that your nervous system processes it in amounts it can handle.[[17]](#ref-17) A scoping review of Somatic Experiencing found preliminary evidence of positive effects on post-traumatic symptoms, from studies of mixed quality.[[18]](#ref-18)

Fear sometimes ends in shaking. Levine observed that animals in the wild often tremble after escaping a threat and then return to normal behavior, and he proposed that humans often suppress the same discharge. If you start to shake or tremble while you feel fear, let it happen. In Tension and Trauma Releasing Exercises (TRE), developed by David Berceli, a short sequence of exercises tires the leg and hip muscles and induces this kind of tremor on purpose.[[19]](#ref-19) TRE has little research behind it so far, so test it for yourself.

If you have a history of severe or complex trauma, work with fear slowly, and consider doing that work with a skilled practitioner, if you can find a good one.

## 6. Integration

A realization is the moment a belief or a protective intent becomes conscious and felt, not just figured out. Integration is what happens afterward: the part stops needing its protective strategy, and its feelings and beliefs rejoin the rest of you. Two techniques make integration more likely.

### Memory reconsolidation

For most of the twentieth century, neuroscientists assumed that a memory, once consolidated, was fixed. In 2000, Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe, and Joseph LeDoux showed that a consolidated fear memory in rats became unstable again when it was reactivated, and that it needed new protein synthesis in the amygdala to stabilize a second time.[[20]](#ref-20) A reactivated memory can therefore be changed before it is stored again. Researchers later found in humans that reactivation alone is not enough: the memory destabilizes only when the reactivation includes a *prediction error*, a mismatch between what the memory predicts and what actually happens.[[21]](#ref-21)

Bruce Ecker, Robin Ticic, and Laurel Hulley turned these findings into a method. First, retrieve the emotional learning behind a symptom and make it vivid enough to feel. Then, while it is active, have an experience that clearly contradicts it, and repeat the contrast a few times. When this works, the old reaction stops arising in situations that used to trigger it, without any effort to suppress it.[[22]](#ref-22) Richard Lane and colleagues have proposed that reconsolidation is the common mechanism of change across many kinds of inner work: an old memory is reactivated, a new emotional experience is incorporated into it, and new behavior reinforces the result.[[23]](#ref-23) The hypothesis remains debated, and the human evidence is less settled than the animal evidence. The practical test is the one the process uses anyway. When a similar situation comes up again, does your behavior change?

### Gentle reparenting

A protective belief is often held by a younger part of you, tied to a memory in which you needed something that no one gave you, such as safety, attunement, comfort, or someone on your side. Gentle reparenting gives that younger part what it needed, with your present adult self as the caregiver. In imagination, you enter the memory, stand with your younger self, and offer the care that was missing.

Reparenting also creates the prediction error that reconsolidation needs. The memory predicts that no one will come, and someone comes. Teal Swan's Completion Process is one structured form of this. In it, you start from an emotion in the present, find the past experience that holds it, and resolve that experience by meeting the needs that went unmet.[[24]](#ref-24) Nicole Johnson describes another form in *Reparenting Your Inner Child*, combining inner child work with IFS and self-compassion.[[25]](#ref-25)

## 7. What to do with this

Start with something small. Choose a moment of mild stuckness, such as an email you keep postponing or a project you want to start and have not, and run the loop once. In small cases you learn how your parts communicate, and the skill transfers to larger ones.

Write down what each part says. Writing keeps the dialogue concrete, and it gives you a record to check later, which is how you find out whether an integration held. The real test comes weeks afterward, when a similar situation arises and you notice that you act differently without trying.

Respect your limits. When you have a direct need to meet, such as sleep, food, an obligation, or your safety, stop, thank the part, and commit to returning to the part. Then keep the commitment, because a part that is promised attention and forgotten learns not to trust you. When fear or grief is too strong, titrate. A process that goes slowly and holds together does more than a process that goes fast and overwhelms you.

You can do this work alone, with an AI model as a patient interviewer and note-taker, or with a skilled practitioner, if you can find a good one. If you think you do not need it, running step 1 takes a few seconds when that is true. If you worry that it means dwelling on the past, notice that the process always starts in the present. You go to a memory only when a part leads you there, and that memory was already affecting your present reaction.

For readers who want to understand parts work in depth, these resources describe the mechanics more precisely than most:

- In "[Building up to an Internal Family Systems model](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/5gfqG3Xcopscta3st/building-up-to-an-internal-family-systems-model)," Kaj Sotala derives IFS from the design of a robot that avoids catastrophes.[[13]](#ref-13)
- In "[Book summary: Unlocking the Emotional Brain](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/i9xyZBS3qzA8nFXNQ/book-summary-unlocking-the-emotional-brain)," Sotala gives the clearest short account of memory reconsolidation as a practice.[[26]](#ref-26)
- In *Self-Therapy*, Jay Earley lays out IFS step by step for people working on their own.[[27]](#ref-27)
- In *No Bad Parts*, Richard Schwartz introduces IFS himself, with exercises.[[12]](#ref-12)
- In the *CFAR Handbook* chapter on Internal Double Crux, the Center for Applied Rationality sets out a procedure for resolving a conflict between two parts.[[14]](#ref-14)
- In *Focusing*, Eugene Gendlin teaches the bodily attention that the rest of the work depends on.[[9]](#ref-9)
- In *Unlocking the Emotional Brain*, Bruce Ecker, Robin Ticic, and Laurel Hulley give the full method of reconsolidation, with session transcripts.[[22]](#ref-22)

## 8. One place

Resistance seems to be the thing that keeps you from what you want. When you trace it to its source, you find a younger version of you that learned something true at the time and never found out that the situation had changed. Once that part is heard and given what it needed, it no longer has to guard you, and the energy it used for guarding becomes available to you again.

Each integration reduces the number of places you act from. Excitement becomes easier to feel, because fewer beliefs distort it, and acting on it becomes easier, because fewer parts object. Hegel thought that the wounds of spirit heal without leaving scars. The parts of you that were split off return to the whole, and they bring what they learned with them.

The next time you feel stuck, ask the first question again.

## Glossary

### States and pulls

<a id="g-highest-excitement"></a>**Highest excitement**: The option that, right now, carries the strongest natural pull of interest and aliveness, with no belief driving it. It's a felt signal, not a judgment about what you *should* do. Cleaning a toilet can be your highest excitement. So can rest.

<a id="g-natural-flow"></a>**Natural flow**: Acting from highest excitement with no inner conflict. The default state when no part is resisting.

<a id="g-attachment"></a>**Attachment**: A pull toward something that comes from a belief (for example, "I need this to be okay"), not from natural flow. It can feel like excitement, but it has a grasping or compulsive quality.

<a id="g-aversion"></a>**Aversion**: A push away from something that comes from a belief (for example, "this means I'm failing"). Choosing something to escape something else is aversion, not excitement.

<a id="g-stuck-or-frustrated"></a>**Stuck or frustrated**: The state in which a specific want meets inner resistance, so energy goes toward the want but doesn't flow.

### The inner landscape

<a id="g-part"></a>**Part**: A semi-autonomous subsystem of the psyche with its own beliefs, goals, and protective strategies. Talking about parts lets you relate to an inner conflict instead of identifying with it. See [Parts](#4-parts).

<a id="g-resistance"></a>**Resistance**: Any felt reluctance, contraction, avoidance, or loss of energy that comes up when you go to act on a want. The process treats it as information from a part, not as an obstacle to force past.

<a id="g-protective-intent"></a>**Protective intent**: The positive purpose behind a part's resistance. The process assumes that every resisting part is doing its best to keep you safe, based on what it believes.

<a id="g-belief"></a>**Belief**: A held assumption about how things are, what something means, or what will happen. Beliefs determine much of what you perceive, feel, and do, including which options seem possible. In this process, attachment, aversion, and resistance are all treated as coming from beliefs.

<a id="g-unconscious-belief"></a>**Unconscious belief**: A belief that affects your feelings and behavior without your awareness. It usually formed earlier in life, and you hold it as simply "how things are."

<a id="g-fear-based-belief"></a>**Fear-based belief**: An unconscious belief whose core is a predicted threat, such as rejection, failure, loss, or harm. It makes possibilities feel dangerous or flat.

<a id="g-hidden-fear"></a>**Hidden fear**: Fear you don't consciously feel, which appears indirectly, most often as numbness, flatness, or nothing feeling exciting.

<a id="g-past-traumatic-memory"></a>**Past traumatic memory**: A memory that still carries an unresolved emotional charge and holds a protective belief in place. It doesn't have to be a major event. Often several memories together produce a belief.

<a id="g-possibility-space"></a>**Possibility space**: The set of options you currently perceive as available for getting what you want. Beliefs limit it, so resolving a belief can reveal options that weren't visible before. The essay on [consideration space](/consideration-space) covers it in depth.

### Signals

<a id="g-anxiety"></a>**Anxiety**: An uneasy signal that may hold useful information about an action you are resisting. It usually involves either a negative prediction about the future or a fear that a negative past scenario will happen again in the present. Either way, it directs your attention away from the present moment.

<a id="g-distorted-anxiety"></a>**Distorted anxiety**: Anxiety out of proportion to a probable threat. To check, ask whether the anxiety concerns this exact scenario or a similar one from the past, whether the anxiety's intensity matches the consequences if the bad scenario came true, whether you can control the scenario at all, and whether acting to reduce the risk reduces the anxiety in proportion.

<a id="g-fear"></a>**Fear**: A more immediate felt sense of threat, often in the body, best met by letting it run its full course before you do integration.

### Techniques

<a id="g-considering-potentials"></a>**Considering potentials outside the possibility space**: Deliberately generating options beyond your current assumptions, for example by asking "what would I do if this constraint weren't true?" or by imagining how someone very different from you would approach the situation.

<a id="g-feeling-somatically"></a>**Feeling somatically**: Paying attention to sensations in the body, such as tightness, heaviness, contraction, or temperature, to find emotions or beliefs before you can put them into words.

<a id="g-feeling-the-fear-fully"></a>**Feeling the fear fully**: Letting fear be present in the body, without resisting, analyzing, or acting on it, until its intensity peaks and subsides.

<a id="g-titration"></a>**Titration**: Feeling a strong emotion in small doses, alternating with attention to something that feels safe or calm, so that it doesn't overwhelm you.

<a id="g-curiosity-based-dialogue"></a>**Curiosity-based dialogue**: An inner conversation with a part in which you ask, without agenda or judgment, what it's protecting you from, what it expects would happen, and why its behavior seems beneficial.

<a id="g-integration-technique"></a>**Integration technique**: Any method that resolves an emotional learning at its root, so that the part no longer needs its protective strategy. In this process: memory reconsolidation and gentle reparenting.

<a id="g-memory-reconsolidation"></a>**Memory reconsolidation**: The brain's process for updating an emotional memory. When the memory and its belief are vividly active and you experience something that clearly contradicts them, the old learning can be rewritten, not just suppressed. Many techniques use this process.

<a id="g-gentle-reparenting"></a>**Gentle reparenting**: Giving a younger part of yourself, usually one tied to a past memory, the safety, attunement, and care it didn't get then, with your present adult self as the caregiver.

### Outcomes

<a id="g-realization"></a>**Realization**: The moment an unconscious belief or protective intent becomes consciously understood and felt, not just figured out.

<a id="g-acknowledged"></a>**Acknowledged (part)**: A part feels heard and understood, with its protective intent recognized. You usually feel this as relief or softening.

<a id="g-integration"></a>**Integration**: The healing of fragmentation. A part that split off, holding feelings and beliefs at odds with the rest of you, rejoins the whole, so you act from one place instead of from inner conflict. It begins after a realization, when the part feels acknowledged. The real test comes later: when similar scenarios come up, does your behavior change?

### Pausing

<a id="g-direct-needs"></a>**Direct needs**: Immediate practical or physical needs, such as sleep, food, obligations, or safety, that justify pausing the process.

<a id="g-commit-to-return"></a>**Commit to return**: An explicit promise to the part that you will come back to it later, which keeps its trust when you pause before it's resolved.
